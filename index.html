Why Job Descriptions Matter to Recruiters

A JD is not just a list of requirements — it's a treasure map. Every section hides clues about the ideal candidate, the team culture, the urgency, and the sourcing strategy.

📖 REAL SCENARIO

The Recruiter Who Read Between the Lines

Neha, a first-year healthcare recruiter, received a JD for a "Travel ICU RN — 2+ years ICU experience, BLS, ACLS, Epic EMR." Her colleague sourced the obvious: LinkedIn, 2yr ICU RN, US-based. 20 submittals. Zero interviews.

Neha re-read the JD. She noticed: "Texas NLC Compact license preferred" in the requirements. "Epic EMR strongly preferred" buried in a bullet. "Start within 30 days" in the assignment details. She narrowed her search to compact-licensed ICU nurses with Epic experience available immediately. 5 submittals. 3 interviews. 1 placement.

🏆 The lesson: Every word in a JD is a sourcing signal. The recruiter who reads deeply wins. This module teaches you exactly how to do that.

"A job description is a recruiter's blueprint. Miss a line, miss the hire. Read every word — even the ones that seem generic — because each one tells you something about who this company is and who they really need."

— Senior Technical Recruiter, Fortune 500 Staffing Agency
🔬

Reading vs Decoding

Reading a JD means skimming the title and skills list. Decoding means analyzing every section for hidden signals: urgency, culture fit, team structure, real vs. stated requirements, and compensation clues.

Core Skill
🗂️

Must-Have vs Nice-to-Have

Most JDs mix hard requirements with wishlist items. Your job: separate them. Submitting a candidate missing a must-have kills the submittal. Missing a nice-to-have rarely does.

Critical Distinction
🏢

Company Context

The company description section tells you the work environment, growth stage, and culture. A community hospital JD reads very differently from a Level I Trauma Center or health system JD — even for the same nursing title.

Often Overlooked
⚡

Speed vs Accuracy Trade-off

In staffing, speed matters — but submitting the wrong candidate is worse than submitting late. A 15-minute deep JD analysis saves hours of wasted outreach and damaged client relationships.

Anatomy of a Job Description

Every JD has a structure. Learn to recognize each section and what it tells you. Click any section below to expand its sourcing significance.

📋 Standard JD Structure — Annotated

Click each section to understand what it reveals about the ideal candidate

💼
Job Title & Level MUST READ
Position name, seniority, department
Section 1

What it reveals: Level of experience required, reporting structure, and team fit. "Senior" vs "Lead" vs "Principal" have very different candidate profiles. Always verify: Is the title standard in the market? "Ninja" and "Rockstar" titles often mean startup culture and flexible requirements.

Sourcing action: Search by alternate titles. A "ICU RN" at one health system = "Critical Care Nurse" or "MICU RN" elsewhere. Boolean: "ICU RN" OR "Critical Care RN" OR "CCRN" OR "Intensive Care Nurse"

🏢
Company Overview SOURCING INTEL
Company mission, size, stage, industry
Section 2

What it reveals: Company stage (startup vs enterprise), industry niche, culture, and what kind of person thrives there. A community critical access hospital needs someone very different from a Level I Trauma Center, even for the same nursing title.

Sourcing action: Google the company before sourcing. Check Glassdoor reviews, LinkedIn company page, Crunchbase funding. This shapes your candidate pitch: "They're a Level I Trauma Center, nationally ranked, with a 1:2 ICU ratio and full benefits on Day 1" is more persuasive than just the role title.

📝
Role Summary / About the Role MUST READ
What the person will do day-to-day
Section 3

What it reveals: The real job — beyond the title. A "Registered Nurse" role summary saying "manage 1:1 ratio on ECMO patients" tells you: they need someone with advanced critical care competency, not just general ICU experience.

Sourcing action: Extract 2-3 core themes from this section. These become your primary LinkedIn/ATS search filters. Day-to-day activities reveal the true environment and pace.

✅
Responsibilities / Key Duties MUST READ
Bullet-point list of what they'll own
Section 4

What it reveals: The weight and priority of each function. Bullets listed first = higher priority. If "managing a team of 5" is bullet 1, leadership is non-negotiable. If it's bullet 8, it's secondary.

Sourcing action: Map the top 3 responsibilities to candidate experience. Build your screening questions from this section. Ask: "Tell me about your experience managing ventilator-dependent patients" only if that responsibility is explicitly listed.

⭐
Required Qualifications MUST MATCH
Non-negotiable must-haves for the role
Section 5

What it reveals: Hard filters for the role. Missing one = automatic rejection in most cases. Includes: years of experience, specific tools/technologies, education, certifications, work authorization.

Sourcing action: Build your Boolean search string directly from this section. Every keyword here is a non-negotiable filter. Never submit a candidate who is missing a required qualification without first flagging it to your manager or client contact.

Common trap: "5+ years experience" — is this truly required, or aspirational? Ask the hiring manager. Many will accept 3.5 years of exceptional candidates.

➕
Preferred Qualifications NICE TO HAVE
Bonus skills that strengthen candidacy
Section 6

What it reveals: The dream candidate profile. These are differentiators — candidates who have them rise above others. Also reveals the company's growth direction: "experience with AI/ML pipelines" as preferred = they're building in that direction.

Sourcing action: Use these as tiebreakers when comparing similar candidates. If two candidates match all requirements equally, the one with preferred qualifications wins. Mention them in your submittal notes to the client.

💰
Compensation & Benefits SOURCING INTEL
Salary, benefits, remote/hybrid policy
Section 7

What it reveals: Market positioning, candidate quality level, and deal-breakers. A role paying $85K-$100K attracts very different candidates than one paying $120K-$150K. Remote/hybrid policy affects your candidate pool dramatically.

Sourcing action: Cross-reference against market data (Medscape Compensation Report, MGMA, Glassdoor, LinkedIn Salary, Bureau of Labor Statistics). If the pay is below market for the specialty, flag it early — it will be your biggest rejection reason. Travel = national pool. Perm onsite = local pool only.

🌟
Culture / EEO / About Us CANDIDATE PITCH
Company values, work style, diversity statement
Section 8

What it reveals: The "sell" — what makes this company attractive to candidates. Words like "mission-driven," "flat hierarchy," "unlimited PTO," "diverse and inclusive" are your pitch points when cold-reaching candidates.

Sourcing action: Pull 2-3 culture points into your outreach message. Passive candidates don't apply for job descriptions — they respond to compelling narratives. "They have a 5-Star CMS rating, a 1:2 nurse-to-patient ratio, and pay Day 1 medical benefits" gets responses. A dry job title does not.

The 8 Key Elements — Deep Dive

Each element of a JD requires a different analytical lens. Here is how to extract maximum sourcing value from each.

The Problem: Job titles are not standardized across companies. "Senior Engineer" at a 10-person startup ≠ "Senior Engineer" at Microsoft. Search only by title = miss 60% of qualified candidates.

The Fix — Title Normalization: For every JD title, generate 3-5 equivalent titles used in the market:

  • ICU RN → Critical Care RN, CCRN, Intensive Care Nurse, MICU Nurse, CCU Nurse
  • Physical Therapist → PT, DPT, Inpatient PT, Acute Care PT, Physiotherapist
  • Director of Nursing → DON, DNS, Director of Clinical Services, ADON (step-up candidate), VP of Nursing
  • Registered Nurse (Med/Surg) → Medical-Surgical RN, MS RN, Step-Down RN, General Care RN

Boolean tip: Always use OR to capture alternates: ("Critical Care RN" OR "ICU Nurse" OR "CCRN" OR "Intensive Care Nurse" OR "MICU RN")

Stated: "5+ years of experience required." Real: Often flexible. Here's how to interpret experience requirements:

  • "1-3 years" — Junior to early-mid level. Recent grads with strong internship experience qualify.
  • "3-5 years" — Mid-level. Core competency expected. Negotiable by 6-12 months with strong portfolio.
  • "5-8 years" — Senior level. Usually firm for IC roles, sometimes negotiable for lead roles.
  • "10+ years" — Leadership/Principal/Architect level. Years matter less than impact and scope.

Always ask the hiring manager: "Is the years-of-experience requirement firm, or is it a guideline?" You will often be told they're open to exceptional candidates with fewer years.

Technical skills in a JD fall into three buckets — and confusing them kills placements:

  • Must-Have (Day 1): Core clinical skills, licenses, or EMR systems used immediately. Cannot train on these. If the JD requires 2 years of ICU experience and Epic proficiency — these are non-negotiable patient safety standards.
  • Preferred (Can Learn): Tools used occasionally or being adopted. A strong engineer picks these up quickly. Missing them shouldn't disqualify.
  • Trainable (Good-to-Have): Ancillary tools, platforms, or frameworks. Mentioned once. The company will train.

How to categorize: Look at where the skill appears. In Required section + mentioned in Responsibilities = Must-Have. Only in Preferred = can be learned. Mentioned once in company description = trainable.

Location types and their impact on sourcing pool:

  • 100% Onsite: Local candidates only. Narrow pool — compensate with higher urgency and faster process.
  • Hybrid (2-3 days/week): Commutable distance — typically within 50-60 miles of office.
  • Remote (US-based): National pool. Massive advantage — highest quality candidates available.
  • Remote (specific states): Check state tax/compliance requirements. Some companies avoid certain states.

Work Authorization: "Must be authorized to work in the US" = No H-1B sponsorship. "Will sponsor" = H-1B eligible candidates welcome. For healthcare: always verify compact license state for travel nurses.

The reality about education requirements in tech and many industries:

  • "Bachelor's degree required" — In tech: 70% of companies waive this for candidates with 5+ years experience and strong portfolio. Always ask.
  • "Bachelor's in Computer Science or related field" — "Related field" is very broad. Information Systems, Mathematics, Engineering all qualify. Bootcamp grads with experience often qualify.
  • "Master's degree preferred" — Almost always optional. Only truly required in research-heavy, academic, or specialized science roles.
  • Healthcare exception: Degrees and certifications are strictly regulated by law. An RN license is non-negotiable. A CRNA must have a DNP-A. No flexibility.

Even when salary isn't listed, the JD gives you clues:

  • Company size + role title → Glassdoor/LinkedIn Salary range
  • Community hospital + "Registered Nurse" → typically $65K-$80K (market rate)
  • Level I Trauma Center + same role → $80K-$100K + shift diff + sign-on bonus
  • "Competitive compensation" → Usually market rate; always benchmark before calling candidates
  • Travel contract → base + tax-free stipends; understand the tax home rule before discussing pay

Key tools: Medscape Compensation Report (healthcare), MGMA (physician/clinical), Glassdoor, LinkedIn Salary, BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook. Always benchmark before your first candidate conversation so you can set expectations accurately.

JDs telegraph urgency without saying it explicitly. Look for these signals:

  • "Immediate start" → Active role, client is losing productivity. Prioritize this JD.
  • "We are a fast-growing team" → Scaling rapidly; likely multiple headcount being added.
  • "Re-posted" or "Updated X days ago" → Previously failed to fill. Find out why — price? requirements? market availability?
  • Very specific technical requirements → Either had a bad hire they're replacing, or a very specific project need. Ask what triggered the opening.
  • "Backfill" in internal notes → Someone resigned. Urgency is high.

Ask the hiring manager: "What happens to the team if this role isn't filled in 30 days?" The answer tells you the real urgency and gives you leverage in candidate conversations.

Domain experience is often implied, not stated explicitly:

  • "Experience with HIPAA compliance" → Healthcare industry background required
  • "CMS survey experience" → SNF/LTC regulatory background required
  • "Joint Commission standards" → Accredited hospital or ambulatory care setting
  • "Experience in regulated environments" → Healthcare, pharma, home health, or government
  • "OASIS / RAI / MDS familiarity" → Home health or SNF clinical documentation expertise

Sourcing action: Add domain terms to your Boolean: ("Registered Nurse" AND ("ICU" OR "critical care" OR "MICU") AND "HIPAA"). Domain-matched candidates get shortlisted faster — even with slightly fewer years of experience.

The "So What" Test

After reading each JD bullet, ask "so what does this mean for sourcing?" Every requirement must translate to a concrete filter or keyword. If it doesn't affect your search, it's background context.

Framework
🚩

Red Flags in JDs

"Wearing many hats," "fast-paced chaos," "must be OK with shifting priorities," "no micromanagement." These signal organizational issues. Not disqualifying — but mention them honestly to candidates.

Watch Out
💬

Ask 5 Questions First

Before sourcing: 1) Why is this open? 2) Who failed last time? 3) What's the timeline? 4) What's the team structure? 5) Is the comp flexible? These answers shape everything.

Memorial Hermann — Houston Medical Center is a 926-bed Level I Trauma Center and teaching hospital affiliated with UTHealth Houston. Ranked among the top hospitals in Texas for critical care, cardiac, and neurology.

13-week travel contract in the Medical ICU (MICU). Three 12-hour night shifts per week (7pm–7am). Start date: within 30 days of contract signing. 1:2 nurse-to-patient ratio.

🚨
CRITICAL: LICENSE & COMPACT STATUS
Texas is an NLC Compact state. A nurse with a Compact home state license can practice immediately. Non-compact licenses (CA, NY, NJ) require a separate TX license — takes 4–8 weeks. Ask license state on first call.
Day 1 Screen
⏱️
URGENCY: 30-DAY START
"Within 30 days" = high urgency. Factor in: compliance/credentialing (2–3 weeks), drug screen, background check. You effectively need a candidate ready to start credentialing immediately. Only submit if they can meet this timeline.
Priority Role
💰
COMPENSATION BREAKDOWN
$3,200–$3,800/wk. Of this, approx. $1,400 housing + $500 M&I = $1,900 tax-free. Taxable base approx. $38–$45/hr. IRS tax home rule: candidate must maintain permanent residence elsewhere to receive stipends. Verify tax home status.
Compliance
🔍
CARES SCREENING FRAMEWORK
Credentials: RN lic + BLS + ACLS valid? Availability: Night shift 7p-7a, start in 30 days? Reliability: No disciplinary actions? Experience: 2yr ICU in last 3 yrs, Epic? Salary: Expectations vs $3,200–$3,800/wk?
🚩
COMMON PITFALLS
ICU experience must be within last 3 years — a nurse with 10yr ICU experience but 4yr gap is disqualified. ACLS must not be expired. Epic experience "strongly preferred" = near must-have at this facility. Night shift (7p–7a) is a hard schedule — confirm comfort with nights.

🔍 Recruiter's Decoding Analysis

🎯
THE REAL NON-NEGOTIABLES
Active IL LPN license + BLS + SNF/LTC or acute care experience + PCC familiarity. New grads with strong SNF clinical rotations may qualify despite the 1-year requirement — ask the DON. PCC proficiency is near-mandatory; probe it specifically during screening.

TUITION ASSISTANCE = RETENTION PITCH

Pitch Point
🔍
BOOLEAN SEARCH STRING

LPN (Licensed Practical Nurse) and LVN (Licensed Vocational Nurse) are the same credential — LVN is the title used in California and Texas, LPN everywhere else. Always search both: ("LPN" OR "LVN"). Candidates from TX or CA may list LVN on their resume even when applying to IL roles.
Sourcing Angle
🚩
LICENSE & SCOPE OF PRACTICE CHECK
Illinois is an NLC Compact state. LPNs from compact states can work immediately. Verify the candidate's LPN license covers IV therapy and medication administration — scope varies by state. Some LPNs are not IV-certified; this facility requires full med administration. Confirm on first call.
Watch Out

🎯
THE REAL NON-NEGOTIABLES
Active TX CNA on state registry + clean background check + physical ability to transfer residents. Day 1 Screen
💡

Strategy
🔍
BOOLEAN SEARCH STRING
("CNA" OR "Certified Nursing Assistant" OR "Nurse Aide" OR "Nursing Assistant") AND ("assisted living" OR "memory care" OR "ALF" OR "SNF") AND ("Texas" OR "Dallas")
⚡
MEMORY CARE = SPECIALTY PREMIUM
"Immediate openings on the memory care unit" signals urgency and a specialized environment. Candidates with dementia/Alzheimer's experience are rarer and this facility may flex the rate for the right person. High Priority
🚩
REGISTRY & BACKGROUND CHECK
Texas requires CNAs to be listed on the state Nurse Aide Registry. A disqualifying entry (abuse, neglect, or misappropriation finding) is a federal bar from Medicare/Medicaid-certified facilities. Always verify registry status and ask about background history on the first call — this facility works with a vulnerable population and will screen thoroughly.
